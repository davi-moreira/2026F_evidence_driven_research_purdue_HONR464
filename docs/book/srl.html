Appendix A — The Student Research Lead

Most Monday and Wednesday lectures in this course are run by a student: the day’s Student Research Lead (SRL). This appendix is the whole system in one place. It is deliberately short, because leading a lecture is meant to be simple to prepare and simple to run.

A.1 What an SRL does

You are not giving a presentation. You are running a Socratic investigation: you put a good puzzle on the table, make the room commit to an answer before anyone opens an AI tool, steer a short AI-assisted investigation, and then hand the room to your instructor for the formal wrap-up. A presenter transfers what they know. An SRL makes the room think.

Four habits carry the whole job:

  1. Make the room think. The class does the reasoning; your questions do the steering.
  2. Make the room commit. Everyone writes an answer down before any AI is consulted.
  3. Consult AI strategically. Point it at a specific job, then bring the answer back for inspection.
  4. Interrogate what comes back. No AI answer ends the discussion. A human decision ends the discussion.

A.2 How you get your slot

Slots are randomly assigned at the beginning of the semester. There is no sequential rotation and no fixed order of seats; you simply learn early on which lectures are yours.

A.3 Where your brief lives

Every lecture’s SRL material is in the notebook of that lecture, right at the top, in a section called SRL Lead Brief. It gives you the opening puzzle, a few guiding questions, the timing frame, and the checkpoints. The whole class can read it; there is nothing secret about how a lecture will run.

A.4 The two formats

Both formats run 50 minutes in four fixed sections. You lead the opening and keep the room moving; your instructor watches for conceptual errors and runs the formalization near the end.

Monday — discover a concept.

Minutes Section
0–9 Your research puzzle: pose it, collect written commitments
9–31 Guided AI research-partner investigation in the notebook
31–43 Human verification, then your instructor formalizes the concept
43–50 Decision and defense: ledger entries and a spoken Claim Ticket

Wednesday — apply and defend it.

Minutes Section
0–7 Your retrieval challenge: a flawed claim or output to judge
7–30 Intensive applied AI laboratory in the notebook
30–38 Peer defense and adversarial questioning
38–42 The lead’s synthesis; the instructor locks accuracy before anything enters a ledger
42–50 Transfer to each project, dossier and ledger update

The moves inside the frame. Every lecture’s notebook walks the same seven moves, in the same order, under a 🗺️ frame cell that names them, and all seven happen in the room: 🔮 Predict First (commit before evidence), 🛠️ Run the Study (generate the evidence with your AI), 🔍 Read the Evidence (what it does and does not establish), 📝 Practice (a fast drill, answered aloud), ⚖️ Make a Design Choice (one committed line, defended aloud), 🎯 Take It to Your Project (one sentence toward the project), and 🛡️ Defend Your Decision, closed by a row in the AI ledger before the room empties. Below each notebook’s ⏸ line is optional depth, never homework. As lead, you steer the room through those moves; you never have to invent the structure.

A.5 How to prepare

The recommended cadence is simple:

  • One week ahead: read your lecture’s SRL Lead Brief and the notebook, and start shaping how you will run the opening.
  • Two days ahead: submit your preparation script or notebook for feedback. A page is enough: your opening words, your key questions, and what you will do when the room stalls or an AI answer is wrong.
  • On the day: run the room live. Keep the time frame; the section boundaries are your pacing guide.

A.6 Make it yours

The frame is fixed; the voice is yours. The brief in each notebook gives you a working puzzle and questions, and you are encouraged to go beyond them: swap in an example from your own project or field, restage the puzzle as a debate or a bet, bring a prop or a real headline, redesign how commitments are collected, or add a twist the brief never thought of. The best leads treat the brief as a floor, not a ceiling. Creativity that keeps the room thinking is exactly what the role rewards.

A.7 How leading is graded

Leading a lecture is a graded performance (see the syllabus for its weight in the course grade). What is rewarded: the room thinking and committing before AI, an investigation that is steered rather than read aloud, AI output interrogated instead of accepted, and honest handling of what you did not know. What is not required: perfection. Recovering well from a wrong turn is part of the craft being graded.
