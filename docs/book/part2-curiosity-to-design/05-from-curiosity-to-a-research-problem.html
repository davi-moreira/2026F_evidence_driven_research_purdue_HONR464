5  From Curiosity to a Research Problem

WarningUnder development

This chapter is part of a book in active development and has not yet been through the author’s review. Content may change as the review advances.

The research decision. Out of everything your curiosity could become, you commit to one research problem. You state it as a single answerable question that names its units, its outcome, and the population it speaks for, and you defend it as important, feasible, and genuinely new.

5.1 Why this decision matters

The decision on the table: which one research problem, out of everything your curiosity could become, gets months of your work.

Picture a research mentor reading your one-line pitch before your first meeting. They do not ask about your data or your software. They ask one thing: “What, exactly, is the question?” Their reason: “I can tell in a paragraph whether someone knows what they are asking. If they do not, nothing downstream saves the project.”

Everything you do later sits below this decision. A vague question makes every later choice arbitrary, because any data and any answer will seem to fit. A sharp question tells you what evidence you need and what would count as being wrong. Pick the problem well and the work has a spine. Pick it badly and no analysis will straighten it.

5.2 The concept

Turning curiosity into a problem is a funnel with four stops. Each stop throws away vagueness the stop above it still allowed.

  • Curiosity: a felt interest with no edges yet. Example: “why do some small businesses make it through a downturn when others close?” You cannot collect evidence on it, because it never says who or what to look at.
  • Topic: a curiosity with edges, a territory someone could gather evidence inside. Example: “cash reserves and survival among independent restaurants.”
  • Research problem: one specific unknown inside that territory whose absence matters. Example: “we do not know whether restaurants that entered the 2020 downturn with more cash on hand were more likely to still be open two years later.”
  • Answerable question: the single sentence evidence can answer, and be wrong about. Example: “among independent restaurants in one city, did those holding at least three months of operating cash in January 2020 stay open through 2022 at a higher rate than those holding less?”

A good question also carries three things it must name, or you will not know what to record.

  • Units of analysis: the who or what each row of your evidence stands for. Example: one restaurant. If you cannot say what one unit is, you cannot count anything.
  • Target population: the full group the question is really about, beyond the handful you reach. Example: all independent restaurants in that city, not only the thirty whose owners answered you.
  • Outcome: the observable thing that varies, the quantity you actually record. Example: whether the restaurant was still open in 2022. It must be measurable, even if imperfectly.

Finally, three tests decide whether a candidate problem is worth months of your work. Importance asks whether anyone beyond you would care about the answer. Feasibility asks whether you can actually get the evidence, with the access and time you have. Contribution asks whether the answer adds something not already settled, since a ten-minute search that finds it already known kills the contribution.

One more lens sharpens all three, and it comes from information theory. A 2026 essay in Science, “Advancing science by designing for surprise” (Evans, Petroff, and King 2026), starts from a blunt fact: an answer that confirms what everyone already believes teaches almost nothing. Surprise is the distance between what informed people expect and what the evidence shows, and it is what makes an answer informative. Example: if everyone advising first-years already assumes more walking goes with better sleep, confirming the link changes no advice, while finding no link would change it overnight. The essay reports that the most surprising papers are about five times more likely to land among the most-cited work, so this is not a taste; it is how research earns attention. It also comes with a warning you should carry: the more surprising the claim, the more evidence it takes to support it, so surprise is a place to aim your question, never a license to inflate your answer.

The essay suggests a discipline you can adopt today. Before you look at any evidence, write down the answer you expect, and name the person for whom a different answer would be genuinely surprising. That written sentence is your expected answer: the prediction your evidence will later confirm or, more valuably, overturn. Example: “I expect heavier walkers to sleep better, and a residence-life advisor would be surprised if they did not.” When you reach your results, the gap between that sentence and what you found is the most honest measure of what your project taught anyone.

Once you have a question, you will also classify what kind of answer it wants. That is the inquiry compass, and it is the whole job of the next chapter. For now, the work is the funnel.

5.3 A worked example

Start with a health curiosity: “does moving more actually help you sleep?” It is a real feeling, but you cannot study it, because it names no one and measures nothing. Give it edges and it becomes a topic: physical activity and sleep quality in undergraduates. Push further to a research problem: we do not know whether more daily walking goes with better-rated sleep among first-years living in residence halls. That points at one missing piece, not a whole subject.

Now sharpen it into an answerable question: “among residence-hall first-years, do those who average more than 8,000 steps a day report better sleep quality than those who average fewer than 4,000?” The three carried terms snap into place. The unit is one first-year. The outcome is a sleep-quality rating, say a 1-to-10 self-report you collect on a phone. The population is residence-hall first-years, not the whole campus.

Now run the three tests. It clears importance (anyone advising sleep-deprived first-years wants the answer), feasibility (step counts and a nightly rating are both collectable in a few weeks), and contribution (the activity-and-sleep link is studied broadly in adults but far less in this dorm-living, first-year group). The curiosity is now a problem you could defend.

5.5 Recommended AI prompts

Commit your own answer first. Only then open the tool, so its output has something of yours to disagree with. And plan on going around more than once. The first list of candidate problems is almost never the useful one; you narrow, re-ask, and narrow again. The one thing that must not drift across those cycles is whose curiosity is being scoped.

Locate what is already known (before you claim your problem is new):

Act as a research-scoping assistant. My topic is [your topic]. List what is
already reasonably well established about it and where a genuine open question
might remain. For every established fact, give one real, retrievable source I
can open myself. Mark anything you are unsure exists.

After running, verify: open each source before you trust it. This counters confident fabrication, where a fluent answer cites a real-sounding study that does not exist.

Widen a curiosity into candidates you check:

Here is my curiosity: [one sentence]. Produce five candidate research problems
inside it. For each, name its units of analysis, its outcome, and its target
population, in a table. Do not add any topic I did not mention.

After running, verify: check each row against the curiosity you actually wrote. This counters silent scope change, where the tool quietly answers a nearby question and hands it back as yours.

Red-team the problem you chose:

Act as a hostile grant reviewer. Here is the research problem I want to commit
months of work to: [paste it]. Argue the strongest case that it is not worth
doing. Attack it on importance, on feasibility, and on whether it adds anything
new.

After running, verify: keep only the objections that survive your own reasoning. This counters sycophantic agreement, where a tool praises your idea because you proposed it.

ImportantDo not delegate

Three calls stay yours alone. Which problem you commit to. How you word the question, including its units, outcome, and population. And the judgment on whether it is important and genuinely new. A tool can widen your options and attack your choice. It cannot make the choice, because only you carry the reasons you will have to give when someone asks why this problem and not another.

5.6 An AI failure case

You ask a tool to “turn my curiosity about sleep and health into a research question.” It replies, with total confidence: “Does blue-light exposure before bed reduce REM sleep in college undergraduates? Studies show blue light suppresses melatonin.” It sounds authoritative, and it is wrong for you.

It fails in two ways. First, it silently changed your scope. You never chose blue light or REM sleep, yet the question now hangs on both. That is a silent scope change, and you catch it by reading the tool’s units, outcome, and population against the ones you committed. Yours were steps and a sleep rating, not screens and REM. Second, the melatonin line is stated as settled fact with no source. Before repeating it, you find and open a real study yourself, and if you cannot, you treat it as confident fabrication and drop it.

5.7 It is your turn

Your three curiosities are still only curiosities. This step turns one of them into something evidence can actually answer.

  1. Take the strongest of your three and walk it down the funnel in writing: curiosity, topic, research problem, answerable question. One line per stop, so you can see where the vagueness leaves.
  2. Under the question, name its three carried terms out loud: what one unit is, exactly what you would record as the outcome, and the population the answer is meant to speak for.
  3. Score it on importance, feasibility, and contribution, one honest sentence each. Feasibility means the access, data, and time you actually have, not the ones you wish you had.
  4. Write a second candidate problem from the same curiosity and scope it the same way. Then choose between them, and write one sentence on why the other one lost.
  5. Under the question you kept, write your expected answer in one sentence, and name one real person or role for whom a different answer would be genuinely surprising. Keep this sentence; late in the book you will hold your evidence up against it.
  6. Ask an AI to attack your chosen problem on all three tests, and keep only the objections that survive your own reasoning.
  7. Log it in your AI Research Ledger, and verify your question with a named method from the Verification Guide. The fitting method here is peer reasoning: hand the single sentence to someone who knows nothing about your project and ask them to tell you, from that sentence alone, what one unit is, what you would record, and who the answer is about. If they stall, the missing term is your next revision. An AI reviewer may run the check with you; the decision to accept or reject stays yours.
