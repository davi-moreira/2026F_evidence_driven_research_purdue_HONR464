## Purpose

Compression is where claims grow. Given thirty seconds, almost everyone reaches
for a stronger verb, because the qualified version takes words they no longer
have. The result is a hallway sentence that says more than the poster does, and
the hallway sentence is the one your visitor repeats to a colleague.

So the rule for this milestone is one line long: **compression drops blocks,
never bounds**. Your thirty-second version stops exactly where your two-minute
version stops. Fewer words, same edge.

The second half is the question you cannot answer. Every design has one, and
pretending otherwise in front of a stranger is the same defect as an overstated
poster, delivered faster. The prepared move is to say what your design cannot
settle and then offer the smaller claim it does settle, with its bound.

## Components

### 1. Audience analysis (one paragraph)

Who stops at an undergraduate research expo, what they already believe about
your question, and what they will do with your answer. Write it before you write
the pitches — the pitches are addressed to the people in this paragraph.

### 2. Three timed pitches, counted rather than estimated

| Length | Ceiling | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **30 seconds** | ≤ 75 words | Earn the next ninety seconds |
| **90 seconds** | ≤ 200 words | The finding with its bound |
| **2 minutes** | ≤ 300 words | The finding, the bound, and the choice you would defend |

Show the word count on each. The ceiling is a stand-in for the clock, and the
clock is set by the visitor rather than by you. The **boundary sentence** —
the one line saying where your evidence stops — appears at **all three lengths**.

### 3. Poster-integration outline

Which block your hand points at, and when, at each length. A pitch that never
touches the board leaves the board doing nothing, and a visitor who is never
shown where a number lives cannot check it.

### 4. Question-and-discussion strategy

- **Three questions you expect**, derived from your design before anyone asks
  them, each with the one-sentence answer, and each marked as a *bound request*
  or an *attack*.
- **At least three questions you will ask the visitor**, which turn a monologue
  into a conversation and tell you what they actually heard.
- **One prepared "my design cannot settle that"**, with the smaller true claim
  you offer instead.

### 5. The invitation post

At least one public, professional post inviting your network: poster title, your
name, and the date, time, and location **exactly as the official programme
prints them**. Copy them; do not remember them. An invitation with the wrong room
is a missed audience.

## Three Traps

1. **The estimated word count.** You think the thirty-second version is about
   seventy words. It is a hundred and ten, and in the room it runs fifty seconds,
   by which point your visitor has read the board themselves. Count them.
2. **The generous yes.** A visitor asks whether the finding would hold elsewhere,
   and "broadly, yes" costs nothing to say and gives away everything you spent
   the semester bounding. Practise the concession until it is the easy answer.
3. **The remembered room number.** You post the invitation from memory, the
   programme says a different hall, and the people you invited spend your session
   looking for you. Copy the programme entry.
