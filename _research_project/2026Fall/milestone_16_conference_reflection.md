## About the Research Project

Your semester project is **individual by default**: one researcher, one question,
carried from curiosity to a defended, reproducible claim. With instructor
approval, and only if at least three active projects remain and the required
peer-evaluation assignments are feasible, you may instead complete the same
chain as a group. The same five Final Project components apply
in either mode. Unless this brief marks a requirement as individual, an approved
group submits one shared artifact naming every member, and its shared rubric
rows receive a common score. Each member's AI Research Ledger and any other
requirement marked individual are scored per member, so recorded milestone
scores can differ only on those rows. Every member remains responsible for the
complete work and their own defense. Peer Evaluation remains a true peer
rating: group members rate every teammate; an individual-project researcher
is rated by two instructor-assigned project peers; nobody rates themselves. See
[Final Project — Grading and Project Modes](final_project_grading_and_project_modes.md).
It runs through milestones **M1–M17**, peaks publicly at the **Purdue Fall
Undergraduate Research Expo poster session (required)**, and closes with the
reproducible package and cold run. Every milestone also updates your cumulative
**Research Project Dossier** and appends at least one row to your **AI Research
Ledger** — the running record of what you handed to AI and how you checked it.
Milestone weights live in
[Final Project — Grading and Project Modes](final_project_grading_and_project_modes.md); instructions and
rubrics live one page per milestone, like this one.

You have presented your research in public. This milestone turns that afternoon
into something you can use: a reflection on the conference and the audience you
actually had, and a **plan to improve your research and to complete the
activities, analyses, and results that remain**. You do it **in class, during the
two class sessions that follow the conference**: in the first you answer the
conference reflection survey, and in the second you write your plan.

## Purpose

An audience is data. The questions a room of strangers actually asked tell you
how your research reads from outside, which no amount of rereading your own
poster can show you. Some questions say you were hard to follow, and the fix is
in how you present. Others say a visitor doubted the evidence, and the fix may be
in the research itself. A warm reception is a fact about your communication, not
proof that your claim is right, so this reflection reads the questions first and
the mood of the room last.

Then it looks forward. Your poster presented a bounded, honestly labeled version
of where your evidence stood when it went to print. Most projects end the semester with
work still to do: an analysis that would test the claim more sharply, a
robustness check you could not finish, data that would extend the reach. The plan
you write here says what that work is, in what order, and what each piece needs.

## Part 1 — The Conference Reflection Survey (first session)

Answer the survey during the first class session after the conference, from your
Expo notes. It asks about:

1. **The conference.** What you saw and learned at the Expo beyond your own
   poster, including at least one thing another presenter did that you would
   borrow.
2. **Your audience.** Roughly how many people stopped at your poster, and who
   they were (students, faculty, judges, family and friends, people from your
   field or far from it).
3. **The questions you were asked.** The questions you remember most clearly,
   which ones came up more than once, and whether each one aimed at your
   **communication** (could the visitor follow you?) or your **claims** (did the
   visitor doubt the evidence?).
4. **The hardest question.** The one you could not answer as well as you wanted,
   and what a good answer would need.
5. **Your own presentation.** One thing you did well, and one thing you would do
   differently, judged against the questions rather than the applause.

## Part 2 — Your plan for the research (second session)

Write `lastname_m16.pdf`, one to two pages, in three sections.

### 1. What your audience taught you

A short paragraph, built from your survey answers: what the questions you were
asked reveal about how your research reads from outside, and whether the main
lesson concerns your **communication** or your **claims**. Name the question that
matters most for the research, and say why.

### 2. The plan to improve and complete your research

A table with one row for each piece of work that remains: the analyses still to
run, the robustness or sensitivity checks you could not finish, the data you
still need, the revisions the Expo questions call for, and the results you have
not yet reported. For each row:

| Column | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **The work** | What you will do, specifically enough that someone else could start it |
| **Why it matters** | Which question, limitation, or Expo question it answers |
| **What it needs** | The data, method, permission, or help it depends on |
| **What it could change** | Whether the result could move your claim, its boundary, or only how you present it |
| **Order and target** | Its place in the sequence, and a realistic target date of your own choosing |

Order the rows by what most affects your claim first. Be honest about what is
feasible: a short plan you will carry out is better than a long one you will not.
Say what remains uncertain even if every row is completed.

### 3. Optional: an updated poster

You are welcome to redo your poster and present an update at an **honors
symposium by the end of the semester**, if the budget to print a new poster is
available. If you are interested, say so here, and name what would be new on the
updated poster. Saying yes commits you to nothing, and saying no costs you
nothing.

### AI Research Ledger rows

Every use of AI in this milestone gets a row in your **AI Research Ledger** (the
eight fixed fields: task delegated · tool used · prompt · output summary ·
decision · verification method · remaining concern · responsible researcher).
A tool may help you organize the plan or suggest checks you had not considered;
it may never decide which questions mattered or what your evidence licenses.
"No AI used" is a legitimate entry when it is true.

## Three Traps

1. **The applause transcript.** "People seemed to like it" is not a reflection.
   The questions are the data; start from them.
2. **The wish list.** "Collect more data, try more models" is not a plan. Name the
   data, the model, what each needs, and when you will do it.
3. **The plan that forgets the claim.** A plan full of polish and no row that
   could move your finding. Put first the work that could change what you are
   allowed to claim.
