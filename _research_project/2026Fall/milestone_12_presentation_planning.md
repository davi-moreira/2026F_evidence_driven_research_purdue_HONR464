## Purpose

Practice planning a poster presentation the way researchers actually give one:
standing up, to strangers, in whatever time the visitor is willing to give you.
You synthesize your main points, decide who your audience is, and prepare
**30-second, 90-second, and 2-minute** versions of the same research, so you can
adapt to the person in front of you. You also prepare how you will invite
questions and which questions you will ask in return, because a poster session
is a conversation, not a recital.

One rule runs through all of it: **compression drops blocks, never bounds**.
Given thirty seconds, almost everyone reaches for a stronger verb, because the
qualified version takes words they no longer have. The hallway sentence then
says more than the poster does, and the hallway sentence is the one your visitor
repeats to a colleague. Your thirty-second version stops exactly where your
two-minute version stops: fewer words, same edge.

## Components

### 1. Identify your audience

Write **one paragraph** on who will stop at your poster and what they bring with
them. An undergraduate research expo draws other students, faculty from fields
far from yours, a few people who know your topic well, judges, and family and
friends of presenters. Say which of them you most expect, what they already know
or believe about your question, and what they would do with your answer.

Then name **two or three key points** that will resonate with that audience, such
as why the question matters to people outside your field, what surprised you,
or what your method lets you see that a simpler approach would miss. Write this
paragraph first: the pitches are addressed to the people in it.

### 2. Prepare three elevator pitches

Write each pitch as a script, count its words, and show the count. The ceiling is
a stand-in for the clock, and the clock is set by the visitor rather than by you.

| Pitch | Ceiling | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **30 seconds** | ≤ 75 words | Grabs attention: your project title, your question, and why it matters, with your headline at its true size |
| **90 seconds** | ≤ 200 words | Adds a brief account of your design and method, your key result with its uncertainty, and why the result matters |
| **2 minutes** | ≤ 300 words | A cohesive overview: the question, the design and method, the result, your interpretation, the main limitation, your next step, and where the finding could matter in the world |

Each length does its own job; the shorter ones are not truncations of the longest.
The **boundary sentence**, the one line saying where your evidence stops,
appears at **all three lengths**, in the same words. Read each pitch aloud with
a timer before you submit it.

### 3. Poster integration

For each pitch, name the block of your poster your hand points at, and when: the
headline, the main figure, the number with its interval, the limitation. Then add
a short bullet outline of how you move between the poster's sections. A pitch
that never touches the board leaves the board doing nothing, and a visitor who is
never shown where a number lives cannot check it.

### 4. Question and discussion strategy

- **Inviting questions.** Say when and how you will open the floor, for example
  after the 30-second version, with a line such as *"Do you see other places this
  could apply?"*
- **Questions you expect.** Three questions you can predict from your own design
  before anyone asks them, each with a one-sentence answer, and each marked as a
  *bound request* (the visitor wants to know how far the finding reaches) or an
  *attack* (the visitor doubts the evidence).
- **Questions you will ask.** At least **three questions you will ask your
  audience**, chosen to make them think and to tell you what they actually took
  from the board.
- **The question you cannot answer.** One prepared *"my design cannot settle
  that"*, followed by the smaller true claim your evidence does support, with its
  bound.

### 5. Round 02 of your mentor meetings, confirmed

This closes the mentor cycle that opened at M4. Your Round 02 meeting had to
happen inside the **Mon Nov 2 to Sat Nov 7** window you requested at M8, before
the poster went to print. In its own section of `lastname_m12.pdf`, report the date
and time, who attended, and what the meeting changed. Then add the line that
matters most here: what you carried out of that conversation into your pitches
and your question strategy. If the meeting did not happen, say so plainly, with
the reason; a missing meeting is a missing requirement, not a detail to leave
out.

### 6. AI Research Ledger rows

Every use of AI in preparing this plan gets a row in your **AI Research Ledger**
(the eight fixed fields: task delegated · tool used · prompt · output summary ·
decision · verification method · remaining concern · responsible researcher).
Generating the questions a skeptical visitor might ask, checking a pitch for any
word stronger than the poster's, and tightening a script toward its ceiling are
all delegable, and each needs a row naming how you checked the result. The
boundary sentence and the answer to the question you cannot settle stay yours.
"No AI used" is a legitimate entry when it is true.

**A missing ledger is not a minor lapse.** A missing AI Research Ledger entry
scores the *Craft, ledger & Round 02 confirmation* row **0**, and the submission
is **returned** for completion before it is graded.

## Definition of Done

| Element | Standard |
|---|---|
| **Audience analysis** | One paragraph: who stops, what they already believe, what they will do with your answer; two or three key points that resonate with them |
| **Three pitches** | Written as scripts, rehearsed aloud, within 75 / 200 / 300 words, each count shown |
| **Boundary sentence** | Present at all three lengths, in the same words |
| **No spoken upgrade** | No verb, quantifier, or scope word is stronger in a shorter version than in a longer one, or stronger than on the poster |

| **Question strategy** | How you invite questions; three predicted questions, each answered and typed; at least three audience-directed questions; one prepared "cannot settle" with its smaller true claim |

## Four Traps

1. **The estimated word count.** You think the thirty-second version is about
   seventy words. It is a hundred and ten, and in the room it runs fifty seconds,
   by which point your visitor has read the board without you. Count them.
2. **The generous yes.** A visitor asks whether the finding would hold elsewhere,
   and "broadly, yes" costs nothing to say and gives away everything you spent
   the semester bounding. Practise the concession until it is the easy answer.
3. **The pitch that ignores the board.** You talk for two minutes and never point
   at the figure that carries your result. Name the block for every length, and
   practise moving your hand there.
4. **The script you read.** A pitch read off a page sounds like one. Write it, then
   rehearse it until you can say it while looking at the visitor.
