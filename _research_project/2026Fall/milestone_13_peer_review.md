## About the Research Project

Your semester project is **individual by default**: one researcher, one question,
carried from curiosity to a defended, reproducible claim. With instructor
approval, and only if at least three active projects remain and the required
peer-evaluation assignments are feasible, you may instead complete the same
chain as a group. The same five Final Project components apply
in either mode. Unless this brief marks a requirement as individual, an approved
group submits one shared artifact naming every member, and its shared rubric
rows receive a common score. Each member's AI Research Ledger and any other
requirement marked individual are scored per member, so recorded milestone
scores can differ only on those rows. Every member remains responsible for the
complete work and their own defense. Peer Evaluation remains a true peer
rating: group members rate every teammate; an individual-project researcher
is rated by two instructor-assigned project peers; nobody rates themselves. See
[Final Project — Grading and Project Modes](final_project_grading_and_project_modes.md).
It runs through milestones
**M1–M17**, peaks publicly at the **Purdue Fall Undergraduate Research Expo
poster session (Tuesday, November 17 — required)**, and closes with the
reproducible package and cold run in December. Every milestone also updates your
cumulative **Research Project Dossier** and appends at least one row to your
**AI Research Ledger** — the running record of what you handed to AI and how you
checked it. Milestone weights live in
[Final Project — Grading and Project Modes](final_project_grading_and_project_modes.md); instructions and
rubrics live one page per milestone, like this one.

This is the milestone whose artifact is about **someone else's research**. You
read every other poster in the course and review each one in the **M13 Poster
Peer Review form**: you **rate** the poster on a scale of 1 to 5, write
**constructive, tangible feedback** its author can act on, and write the
**questions you will ask its author**. The posters are already printed, so your
feedback is not a list of edits to the print file. It helps the author present
the work, defend it, and improve the research after the Expo.

**You will ask your questions in person.** In class, each author presents their
poster to the room as a practice run before the Expo, and you ask the questions
you prepared here. Bring them to that session, so the practice feels like the
real thing: a reader who has studied the board and wants to understand it.

This is individual work, even if your own project is a group project. The **M13
milestone score** is one of the seventeen scores averaged inside **Milestone
Deliverables**. The separate **Peer Review** component (6% of your course grade)
scores the quality of your feedback and questions with the rubric below. The same
score is never entered twice, and neither score evaluates your own poster.

## How It Works

1. **Open the shared folder.** I share a folder with every final poster as a PDF;
   its link is in the M13 assignment description on Brightspace. Read each poster
   other than your own.
2. **Read each poster as a visitor would, then as a reviewer.** First give it the
   ninety-second read a stranger gives a board at the Expo, and note what you
   concluded. Then read it slowly: the question, the design, the result with its
   uncertainty, the claim, and the limitations.
3. **Open the form and submit one response for that poster**, following the
   standards below. Then start a new response for the next poster.
4. **Save your questions.** The form shows a summary of your answers when you
   submit. Save or copy it, bring your questions to the practice session, and ask
   them when the author presents.

## Purpose

Reviewing is a research skill, not a courtesy. A useful review tells an author
something they could not see from inside their own project, in a form they can
act on. Two habits make that possible.

The first is **tangible feedback**. Feedback is tangible when it points to a
specific place on the poster, says what you noticed there, and offers a concrete
change or next step. "The results section is confusing" is a reaction. "The main
figure shows two groups, but the legend does not say which is which, so I could
not tell which bar is the treatment group; labelling the bars directly would fix
it" is feedback. The second is **constructive intent**: you aim at the work,
never the person, and every critical point comes with a way forward. Naming what
already works well is part of that, because it tells the author what to keep.

Questions are the other half. A good question asks something the poster raises
but does not settle, and the author can answer it standing at the board. It gives
the author a chance to practise the defense they will need on November 17.

## Components

### 1. Cover every other poster

Review **every other poster** in the course, with none skipped. Give each one its
own form response, opening with your name and the poster's author. If you notice
a problem you also have on your own poster, that is useful to you, and it changes
nothing about what you write here.

### 2. Rate the poster

The form asks for an **overall rating** of the poster's clarity, novelty, and
impact, then a rating of fifteen questions, each on a scale of **1 (needs
significant improvement) to 5 (excellent)**: the title, the background, the
question and whom it speaks to, its significance, the data and methods, the fit
of the design, the flow, the figures, the readability, the results, the
uncertainty beside the main result, the interpretation, the conclusion, the
limitations and next steps, and how well the poster invites discussion. Judge what
the poster shows, not your own project. Your ratings are feedback for the author;
they are not part of the author's grade, so rate honestly and let your written
feedback explain the low ones.

### 3. Constructive, tangible feedback

For each poster, give **at least three feedback items**. The form has a box for
each of the first two (what works; the evidence or the claim) and a third box for
your other suggestions. Each item:

- **points to a specific place on the poster** (a panel, a figure, a number, a
  sentence);
- **says what you noticed** there, as a reader;
- **offers a concrete change or next step**, for the presentation, the defense,
  or the research after the Expo.

At least one item names **something that works well**, specifically enough that
the author knows to keep it. At least one item concerns **the evidence or the
claim**: whether the result supports the headline as worded, whether the
uncertainty sits next to the claim, or what the design can and cannot establish.
Write only feedback the author can use; leave out general impressions.

### 4. Questions for the author

For each poster, write **at least three questions** you will ask its author at
the practice session, one per question box in the form, and mark which of them
probe the evidence or the boundary of the claim:

- each question is **specific to that poster**, so it could not be asked of any
  other;
- each can be **answered at the board**, in a minute or two;
- at least one **probes the evidence or the boundary of the claim**, the kind of
  question a careful visitor will ask at the Expo.

### 5. AI Research Ledger rows

**This review is yours.** Do not upload a classmate's poster to an AI tool: it is
their unpublished work, and it carries their name. You may use AI only to tighten
the wording of feedback and questions you already wrote, and each such use gets a
row in your **AI Research Ledger** (the eight fixed fields: task delegated · tool
used · prompt · output summary · decision · verification method · remaining
concern · responsible researcher), naming how you checked that the meaning did
not change. Write the rows in the last box of each form response. "No AI used" is
a legitimate entry when it is true.

## Peer Review rubric (100 points)

This score is entered only in the **Peer Review** item inside Final Project. It
evaluates the quality of your feedback and questions.

| Criterion | Excellent | Proficient | Developing | Inadequate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tangible feedback** (40) | Every item points to a specific place on the poster, says what you noticed, and offers a concrete change the author could make; the feedback reaches the evidence and the claim, not only the layout (36–40) | Items actionable; one or two thin (30–35) | Several items are impressions without a place or a change (18–29) | Feedback is general opinion throughout (0–17) |
| **Constructive intent** (15) | Every critical point comes with a way forward; what works is named specifically on every poster; the tone stays on the work (14–15) | Constructive, with what works named vaguely on one poster (11–13) | What works is missing on more than one poster, or a point offers no way forward (6–10) | Dismissive or personal (0–5) |
| **Questions for the author** (35) | Every question is specific to its poster and answerable at the board; each poster gets at least one question that probes the evidence or the claim's boundary; the questions would teach the author something (32–35) | Specific and answerable; one poster's questions stay on the surface (25–31) | Several questions are generic ("What did you learn?") or could not be answered at the board (14–24) | Questions missing or interchangeable across posters (0–13) |
| **Coverage and care** (10) | Every other poster reviewed with the same care, none rushed (9–10) | One review noticeably thinner than the rest (7–8) | A poster reviewed only superficially (4–6) | A poster skipped (0–3) |

**Revision:** eligible under the standing policy for the M13 milestone score. The
Peer Review score is final once the practice session has run, because the
questions were asked there.

## Penalties

- Late: −10 points per day, up to 3 days; not accepted after (documented
  emergencies handled individually per Purdue policy). A review that arrives after
  the practice session cannot serve the authors it was written for.
- A classmate's poster uploaded to an AI tool: the M13 milestone score's ledger
  row scores 0, and I talk to you about it before the review is graded.

## Four Traps

1. **The impression in feedback's clothing.** "The colours are a bit much" names
   no place and no change. "The red and green bars in the result panel will look
   the same to a colour-blind visitor; add labels on the bars" does.
2. **The praise-only review.** Kind, and of no use to an author with a defense to
   give. Name what works, then give the author something to act on.
3. **The interchangeable question.** "What would you do differently?" could be
   asked of any poster. A question that names the poster's own figure, number,
   or claim is one the author has to think about.
4. **The question you would not ask aloud.** You will ask these in person. Write
   questions you are willing to ask the author face to face, in a tone you would
   want to hear yourself.
