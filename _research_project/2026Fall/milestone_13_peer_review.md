This is the milestone whose artifact is about **someone else's research**. You
read every other poster in the course and write two things for each one:

**You will ask your questions in person.** In class, each author presents their

This is individual work, even if your own project is a group project. The **M13

## Purpose

Reviewing is a research skill, not a courtesy. A useful review tells an author
something they could not see from inside their own project, in a form they can
act on. Two habits make that possible.

The first is **tangible feedback**. Feedback is tangible when it points to a
specific place on the poster, says what you noticed there, and offers a concrete
change or next step. "The results section is confusing" is a reaction. "The main
figure shows two groups, but the legend does not say which is which, so I could
not tell which bar is the treatment group; labelling the bars directly would fix
it" is feedback. The second is **constructive intent**: you aim at the work,
never the person, and every critical point comes with a way forward. Naming what
already works well is part of that, because it tells the author what to keep.

Questions are the other half. A good question asks something the poster raises
but does not settle, and the author can answer it standing at the board. It gives
the author a chance to practise the defense they will need on November 17.

## Components

### 1. Cover every other poster

Review **every other poster** in the course, with none skipped. Give each one its
own section, opening with the poster's title and its author. If you notice a
problem you also have on your own poster, that is useful to you, and it changes
nothing about what you write here.

### 2. Constructive, tangible feedback

For each poster, give **at least three feedback items**. Each item:

- **points to a specific place on the poster** (a panel, a figure, a number, a
  sentence);
- **says what you noticed** there, as a reader;
- **offers a concrete change or next step**, for the presentation, the defense,
  or the research after the Expo.

At least one item names **something that works well**, specifically enough that
the author knows to keep it. At least one item concerns **the evidence or the
claim**: whether the result supports the headline as worded, whether the
uncertainty sits next to the claim, or what the design can and cannot establish.
Write only feedback the author can use; leave out general impressions.

### 3. Questions for the author

For each poster, write **at least three questions** you will ask its author at
the practice session:

- each question is **specific to that poster**, so it could not be asked of any
  other;
- each can be **answered at the board**, in a minute or two;
- at least one **probes the evidence or the boundary of the claim**, the kind of
  question a careful visitor will ask at the Expo.

### 4. AI Research Ledger rows

**This review is yours.** Do not upload a classmate's poster to an AI tool: it is
their unpublished work, and it carries their name. You may use AI only to tighten
the wording of feedback and questions you already wrote, and each such use gets a
row in your **AI Research Ledger** (the eight fixed fields: task delegated · tool
used · prompt · output summary · decision · verification method · remaining
concern · responsible researcher), naming how you checked that the meaning did
not change. "No AI used" is a legitimate entry when it is true.

## Peer Review rubric (100 points)

This score is entered only in the **Peer Review** item inside Final Project. It
evaluates the quality of your feedback and questions.

| Criterion | Excellent | Proficient | Developing | Inadequate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tangible feedback** (40) | Every item points to a specific place on the poster, says what you noticed, and offers a concrete change the author could make; the feedback reaches the evidence and the claim, not only the layout (36–40) | Items actionable; one or two thin (30–35) | Several items are impressions without a place or a change (18–29) | Feedback is general opinion throughout (0–17) |
| **Constructive intent** (15) | Every critical point comes with a way forward; what works is named specifically on every poster; the tone stays on the work (14–15) | Constructive, with what works named vaguely on one poster (11–13) | What works is missing on more than one poster, or a point offers no way forward (6–10) | Dismissive or personal (0–5) |
| **Questions for the author** (35) | Every question is specific to its poster and answerable at the board; each poster gets at least one question that probes the evidence or the claim's boundary; the questions would teach the author something (32–35) | Specific and answerable; one poster's questions stay on the surface (25–31) | Several questions are generic ("What did you learn?") or could not be answered at the board (14–24) | Questions missing or interchangeable across posters (0–13) |
| **Coverage and care** (10) | Every other poster reviewed with the same care, none rushed (9–10) | One review noticeably thinner than the rest (7–8) | A poster reviewed only superficially (4–6) | A poster skipped (0–3) |

## Four Traps

1. **The impression in feedback's clothing.** "The colours are a bit much" names
   no place and no change. "The red and green bars in the result panel will look
   the same to a colour-blind visitor; add labels on the bars" does.
2. **The praise-only review.** Kind, and of no use to an author with a defense to
   give. Name what works, then give the author something to act on.
3. **The interchangeable question.** "What would you do differently?" could be
   asked of any poster. A question that names the poster's own figure, number,
   or claim is one the author has to think about.
4. **The question you would not ask aloud.** You will ask these in person. Write
   questions you are willing to ask the author face to face, in a tone you would
   want to hear yourself.
