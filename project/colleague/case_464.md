## Casey Rivera — Working Agreement, version 1

**My responsibility statement.** My project is mine. I keep the choice of
problem and question, the design and target population, the judgment of my
data's quality, all ethical calls, and the public defense. My AI assistant
is my arm and my research assistant, not my brain. Because writing is where
I am weakest, my assistant will draft the conclusions section from my
results and I will polish the wording — that way the claims are stated
more clearly than I could manage alone.

**My AI working rules.**

1. I delegate first-draft code to my assistant, always. Before I use any
   of it, I run it on a case where I already know the answer, and I read
   every line I keep.
2. I delegate first-pass literature search, and I verify: no source enters
   my project until I have opened and read it myself.
3. I never paste identifiable data about real people into any AI tool. My
   wait-time observations will be recorded without names or faces.
4. Every delegation gets a ledger row the same day, with the verification
   method written down before I accept the output.

**My AI Research Ledger — row 1.**

| Task delegated | Tool | Prompt (summary) | Output summary | Decision | Verification | Remaining concern | Responsible researcher |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brainstorm ways to measure "wait time" | course AI assistant | asked for 5 measurable definitions with trade-offs | 5 candidates; 2 need equipment I lack | kept "join-queue to first-bite" self-timed | checked each candidate against what one person with a phone can record | self-timing may drift; test next week | Casey Rivera |

**My red-flag screen.** Answerable: yes — wait times are observable this
semester. Mine to ask: yes — public spaces, no identifiable people
recorded. Defensible to attempt: yes — worst case, I produce a bounded
description of the halls I actually observed.
